# Serial Port Speed Caps the Compaq

The owner's suspicion is right: the link between the two machines, not the Raspberry Pi, the 286 or the plasma screen, is what holds the Compaq back, though the culprit is the serial port's top speed and the terminal program sitting on it, not the wire itself. A PC serial port of this era cannot be set faster than 115,200 bits per second, about 11.5 kilobytes a second (KB/s), while even a far slower 4.77 MHz IBM PC can repaint its screen memory at 240 KB/s. Today the link runs at 9600, one-twelfth of that ceiling, through MS-DOS Kermit, which garbled pictures at 57,600 in testing. In an emulated Compaq, a small custom DOS program called PILINK received a full screen at 115,200 with no dots wrong, so the first twelvefold speed-up costs nothing and uses the adapter and cable already owned. Nothing has run on the real machine yet, and its serial chip, believed to be a type with no buffer, could turn out to hold only 38,400 or 57,600. Past serial there are three faster doors: the floppy bay (the GoTEK already owned, an estimated 25-45 KB/s for loading programs), the printer port (a Xircom pocket Ethernet adapter, an estimated 60-90 KB/s, 2026 asking prices $95-382) and the rear expansion connector (Compaq's rare two-slot expansion unit plus a network card, an estimated 200-500 KB/s). Buy in that order, and only after an afternoon of free tests on the real Compaq shows what each port can do.

*Three labels are used throughout. **Measured** means run on 13 October 2026 on the virtual Compaq: the real MS-DOS Kermit 3.16 and the project's own DOS program inside the DOSBox emulator on the Pi, set to roughly the speed of a 12 MHz 286. **Sourced** means a linked document says it. **Estimate** means arithmetic or judgement in the research notes with no measurement behind it. Prices were read on 13 October 2026.*

## The link, not the Pi, the 286 or the plasma, sets the pace

The Pi can do the heavy work in three ways, and they lean on the link differently. As a **remote screen**, the Pi runs the real program (Doom, a web browser, a video player), reduces each picture to amber dots and sends it down the wire; the Compaq only paints dots and reports key presses. As a **network and disk**, the Pi carries network traffic over the serial cable using SLIP (a simple way to run a network connection over a serial line) and serves a file that appears on the Compaq as an extra drive letter. As a **loader**, the Pi delivers native DOS programs such as Doom8088 or the MicroWeb browser, which then run on the 286 itself. No ready-made remote-screen program fits this machine: the one VNC viewer (VNC is a standard remote-screen system) for 8086-class PCs is alpha software from 2004 that draws only on VGA displays ([VNC Viewer 8086](https://vncviewer8086.sourceforge.net/)). The first job therefore needs a custom DOS program, which the project has now written.

The arithmetic behind the owner's suspicion is stark. Serial speed is quoted in baud, here meaning bits per second, and each byte costs ten bits on the wire, so today's 9600 baud moves about 960 bytes a second. One full screen of dots is 16,000 bytes at 640x200 and 32,000 bytes in the plasma's sharper 640x400 mode, so a raw screen takes about 17 or 33 seconds to arrive (arithmetic). The Compaq can paint far faster than that: a 4.77 MHz IBM PC changes screen memory at 240 KB/s ([Trixter](https://trixter.oldskool.org/2014/06/19/8088-domination-post-mortem-part-1/)), and the notes estimate over 1 MB/s for a 12 MHz 286. A PC serial port's speed is set by a whole-number divider, and 115,200 is "divide by one"; the next steps down are 57,600 and 38,400, with nothing in between ([Kermit 3.16 source](https://www.kermitproject.org/msdoskermit/msk316src.zip)). So **even a perfect serial link delivers about one-twentieth of what a 1981 PC could paint, and roughly one-ninetieth of the estimate for this one**. Nothing in the research points to the Pi as a limit for sending frames; its one tight spot is memory, if it has to run a full modern browser to render web pages (estimate).

Two things on the Compaq's side decide how much of that 11.5 KB/s is usable, and neither is the cable. The first is Kermit, the terminal program (software that displays whatever the other computer sends) in use today. Kermit draws every picture itself from sixel data, a way of sending pictures as text characters, and its drawing code sets the pace: in the emulator a 9.2 KB photo took 10.9 seconds at 9600, 2.7 seconds at 38,400, and came out garbled at 57,600 (measured; [rig measurements](../research_notes/Compaq%20Portable%20III%20faster%20links/rig_measurements.md)). Kermit's own documentation agrees, putting its limit at 57,600 on a 386 with a buffered serial chip and lower than that on a 286 ([Kermit 3.14 notes, in msk314.zip](https://www.kermitproject.org/msdoskermit/msk314.zip)). The second is the serial chip, called a UART. A community-drawn schematic of the Portable III shows a 16450 ([serial schematic](https://github.com/raymondh2/CompaqPortableIII/blob/main/compaqportableIII_mainboard/Serial.kicad_sch)), a type with no FIFO (a small built-in queue, 16 bytes long on the later 16550A chip); the schematic's author marks the mainboard drawings as not verified ([repository README](https://github.com/raymondh2/CompaqPortableIII)). A 16450 holds exactly one byte. At 115,200 a new byte lands every 87 millionths of a second, and if the program has not collected the last one, it is lost (arithmetic). Field reports split. A 4.77 MHz IBM portable with a no-buffer chip, cabled to a Raspberry Pi 5 through a USB serial adapter, failed at 115,200 and settled at 38,400 in spring 2026 ([classic-computing.de thread](https://forum.classic-computing.de/forum/index.php?thread/39299-funktionierende-slip-config-zwischen-pc-xt-8088-4-77mhz-und-raspberry-pi5-via-us/)), while a Tandy 1000SX with a 16450 ran 115,200 with handshaking ([VCFed thread](https://forum.vcfed.org/index.php?threads/8088-and-modem-baud-rates-16450-uart.79806/)). **No measurement for a 12 MHz 286 with a 16450 was found anywhere**, so the real ceiling could be 115,200, 57,600 or 38,400.

The USB adapter adds one quirk worth knowing. The owner's null modem cable (a cable that crosses the send and receive wires so two computers can talk directly) is the full-handshake kind, which also crosses the RTS and CTS wires that let the receiver say "wait"; this is called hardware flow control. FTDI's own FAQ says its chips keep sending for up to three more characters after being told to wait ([FTDI FAQ, seen as a search excerpt](https://www.ftdichip.com/old2020/Support/FAQs.htm)), and a chip that holds one byte cannot absorb three. The fix is in the software design: the Pi sends one bounded block, then waits for a go-ahead byte from the Compaq. That is the pacing a 1 MHz Apple IIc uses to receive video at 115,200 ([Colin Leroy-Mira](https://www.colino.net/wordpress/archives/2024/03/11/how-i-stream-video-through-an-apple-iis-serial-port/)). One Pi setting matters for games: the FTDI driver holds back a lone incoming byte for up to 16 milliseconds unless its latency timer is set to 1 ([Linux FTDI driver source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/torvalds/linux/master/drivers/usb/serial/ftdi_sio.c)).

The plasma and the memory limit picture quality and program size, not speed. In graphics modes every dot is either lit or dark; only text mode has a third, dimmer level ([Operations Guide](https://archive.org/details/compaq-portable-3-operations-user-manual); [86Box plasma code](https://github.com/86Box/86Box/blob/master/src/video/vid_cga_compaq_plasma.c)), so shading has to be dithered (faked with patterns of dots). The 640x400 mode is real: it is BIOS mode 40h, uses 32 KB of screen memory in four interleaved blocks, and is switched on by bit 0 of port 23C6h ([Ralf Brown's Interrupt List](https://github.com/cirosantilli/ralf-brown-interrupt-list/blob/master/inter61a/INTERRUP.A); [John Elliott](https://www.seasip.info/VintagePC/compaq3.html)). It doubles the bytes per screen. The 640 KB of memory, with no expanded memory (EMS, an add-on scheme that lets DOS programs reach past 640 KB), limits native programs: MicroWeb truncates large pages when memory runs short ([MicroWeb](https://github.com/jhhoward/MicroWeb)). It does not limit the remote-screen route, where the Compaq-side program is 2.6 KB.

## A free software change buys 12 times today's speed

The first step needs no purchase: the FTDI adapter and null modem cable already owned carry it. Setting Kermit and the Pi to 38,400 baud is a fourfold gain for everything Kermit does, text included (measured in the emulator). The larger gain comes from taking Kermit out of the picture path. Kermit can be told by the Pi to start a DOS program: with `SET TERMINAL APC UNCHECKED` in its settings file, a special command sequence from the Pi made Kermit run a program and return to the terminal screen by itself (measured; the feature is documented in [Kermit's 3.14 update notes](https://www.kermitproject.org/msdoskermit/msk314.zip)). The program it starts is PILINK, which reads the serial port itself, writes straight into screen memory, and reports key presses and releases.

| Test on the virtual Compaq | Bytes sent | Cable speed | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kermit drawing a photo from sixel data | 9,200 | 9600 | 10.9 s |
| Same photo | 9,200 | 38,400 | 2.7 s |
| Same photo | 9,200 | 57,600 | garbled |
| PILINK, full 640x200 screen of random dots (worst case) | 16,014 | 38,400 | 3.87 s, 0 dots wrong |
| Same screen | 16,014 | 57,600 | 2.61 s, 0 dots wrong |
| Same screen | 16,014 | 115,200 | 1.34 s, 0 dots wrong |
| PILINK, typical picture of shapes and patterns | 4,745 | 115,200 | 0.40 s |
| PILINK, 320x200 worst case (dots drawn 2 wide) | 8,013 | 115,200 | 0.66 s |
| PILINK, 160x100 worst case (dots 4 wide, 2 tall) | 2,008 | 115,200 | 0.16 s |

Every row is measured in the emulator ([rig measurements](../research_notes/Compaq%20Portable%20III%20faster%20links/rig_measurements.md)). The PILINK results held with the serial chip's buffer switched off, which is how a 16450 behaves, and with the emulator told to throw away any byte the program was late for. Key presses and releases both arrived; a terminal can report only the press, which is why held keys in games fail through Kermit. A deliberately cut-off command was detected and recovered from, and the two ends negotiated speed changes and fell back when a test pattern did not arrive intact. **Treat the top speed as a best case.** DOSBox is not a real 286 and its serial chip is more forgiving. Its timing also runs slightly fast: 16,014 bytes in 1.34 seconds is 11.9 KB/s, slightly more than the 11.5 KB/s a real wire carries at 115,200, so the real machine needs at least 1.39 seconds for that screen (arithmetic).

For moving pictures the best guide is a working precedent, not the worst-case rows above. Colin Leroy-Mira streams video to an Apple IIc over the same 115,200-baud link and measured 7-8 frames per second (fps) at 280x192 and 14-15 fps at 210x144 ([Colin Leroy-Mira](https://www.colino.net/wordpress/archives/2024/03/11/how-i-stream-video-through-an-apple-iis-serial-port/)). His stream sends only the bytes that changed since the last frame, called a delta, which works out to about a fifth of the screen per frame. Scaling that ratio to the Compaq gives roughly 3.5-3.8 fps at 640x200, about 7 fps for a 320x200 picture and under 2 fps at 640x400 (estimates). Measurements made after the notes were written bear this out. A test film (Big Buck Bunny) run through the project's own encoder against a pretend 115,200-baud cable got **9.3 fps in a calm scene and 6.0 fps in a busy one at 320x200**, and about 13 fps with the rows halved; the same film played end to end through the real PILINK in the emulator averaged 6.5 fps. Doom, streamed from the Pi with keys that can be held, got **3-4 fps at 320x200 and 5-7 fps at 320x100** (measured; [graphics plan](../graphics-research.md)). Every working project of this kind uses ordered dithering, in which each dot depends only on its own position, because the prettier error-diffusion methods change most of the screen every frame and defeat the delta trick ([Trixter](https://trixter.oldskool.org/2014/06/19/8088-domination-post-mortem-part-1/); [MacFlim](https://github.com/fstark/macflim)).

The same cable can carry a network instead. The documented recipe is the EtherSLIP driver version 11.8 on the Compaq, the `slattach` command on the Pi, and mTCP, a free set of DOS network programs that runs on an 8088 with DOS 2.1 or later; driver version 11.7 has a bug that causes crashes and must be avoided ([mTCP manual](http://www.brutman.com/mTCP/download/mTCP_2026-10-10.pdf); [EtherSLIP](https://github.com/mbbrutman/EtherSLIP)). mTCP's NetDrive makes a disk-image file on the Pi appear as a drive letter, limited under MS-DOS 3.1 to 32 MB per image ([NetDrive](http://brutmanlabs.org/mTCP/mTCP_NetDrive.html)). MicroWeb 2.1, a graphical DOS browser, has a 640x400 mode that uses the same BIOS mode 40h, but it speaks only unencrypted HTTP and shows only GIF images, so the Pi must run a proxy; one called WRP renders any modern page on the Pi and serves it as a clickable GIF ([MicroWeb](https://github.com/jhhoward/MicroWeb); [WRP](https://github.com/tenox7/wrp)). By arithmetic SLIP carries at most about 10.7 KB/s of useful data at 115,200 and 5.3 KB/s at 57,600, and no measured figure on any 286 was found. A 300 KB program would take about 30 seconds to load from NetDrive at 115,200 (estimate). The Compaq has one serial port, so Kermit, PILINK and SLIP take turns on it.

## Three faster doors: floppy bay, printer port, expansion connector

Serial stops at 11.5 KB/s whatever is bought, because going faster means changing the timing crystal or the chip on the Compaq's board (estimate). The machine has three other ways in.

### The GoTEK already owned is a 25-45 KB/s loader

A GoTEK is a box that takes the place of a floppy drive and serves disk-image files from a USB stick; FlashFloppy is the free replacement firmware most people run on it. A 1.2 MB floppy spins at 360 revolutions a minute, six a second ([cctalk](https://classiccmp.org/pipermail/cctalk/2016-June/024346.html)), and holds 15 sectors of 512 bytes per track, so it delivers at most 46 KB/s; the notes estimate **25-45 KB/s** in practice, two to four times the serial ceiling (arithmetic and estimate, not measured). The settings with a positive report on a 286 are FlashFloppy's `interface = ibmpc`, the select jumper on S1 only, and disk images of exactly 1,228,800 bytes: on an IBM AT an owner reported those 1.2 MB images reliable and 360 KB images not ([FlashFloppy wiki](https://github.com/keirf/flashfloppy/wiki/Host-Platforms); [FlashFloppy issue 1072](https://github.com/keirf/flashfloppy/issues/1072)). The 1.44 MB format in the GoTEK's model name is out of reach, because Compaq required system ROM revision N.2 or later and MS-DOS 3.31 for it ([Compaq Service Quick Reference Guide](https://www.minuszerodegrees.net/manuals/Compaq/Compaq%20Portable%20III%20-%20Information%20from%20the%20CSQRG.pdf)). Reports from Portable III owners are mixed, including one who could not boot from the GoTEK until the machine had first been started from a real floppy ([VCFed thread](https://forum.vcfed.org/index.php?threads/74838/)).

The Pi Zero W already owned turns the GoTEK into a Wi-Fi floppy. A Pi Zero can pretend to be a USB stick (USB "gadget mode"), which is proven with a FlashFloppy GoTEK ([FlashFloppy discussion 451](https://github.com/keirf/flashfloppy/discussions/451)) and packaged for the Amiga as ADF On-The-Go ([adfotg](https://github.com/Zalewa/adfotg)). Two rules apply. The disk image must not be changed while it is attached, so the Pi ejects it, rewrites it and re-attaches it ([Linux kernel documentation](https://github.com/torvalds/linux/blob/master/Documentation/usb/mass-storage.rst)). The +5 V wire in the USB cable must be cut or blocked, or the Pi feeds power backwards into the GoTEK and the computer ([adfotg](https://github.com/Zalewa/adfotg)). This is a bulk channel, 1.2 MB at a time in either direction, suited to delivering programs and not to live pictures. New GoTEKs most likely contain an Artery chip, which is loaded with FlashFloppy over a USB A-to-A cable ([Gotek models](https://github.com/keirf/flashfloppy/wiki/Gotek-Models); [firmware programming](https://github.com/keirf/flashfloppy/wiki/Firmware-Programming)).

### A CompactFlash card brings the hard disk back

Native programs of any size need the hard disk. The BIOS accepts only a fixed table of 47 drive types, of which Type 2 is Compaq's 20 MB drive and Type 42, at 504 MB, is the largest ([John Elliott](https://www.seasip.info/VintagePC/compaq3.html)). A CompactFlash (CF) card in a CF-to-IDE adapter is a known replacement: one owner and one kit maker run 4 GB cards as Type 42 ([OldSilicon](https://www.oldsilicon.com/compaq-portable-iii); [Stillwell Labs](https://www.tindie.com/products/stillwell/compaq-portable-iii-compact-flash-ssd-hard-drive/)), while a restorer of several machines could make only Type 4 (68 MB) work and insists on industrial cards that present themselves as a fixed disk ([Ret.rocks](https://ret.rocks/index.php/one-off-works/compaq-portable-iii)). MS-DOS 3.1 cannot use more than 32 MB per volume ([classiccmp](https://www.classiccmp.org/mailman3/hyperkitty/list/test-drb@ccmp.vtda.org/message/OYGEUUYBUYLTIKJNHGDJNYB7HBF3QJSU/)), so the simplest first try is a card left at Type 2, which needs no settings change (inference). Changing the drive type means running Compaq's SETUP program from a diagnostics diskette, because SETUP is not in the ROM ([Ret.rocks](https://ret.rocks/index.php/one-off-works/compaq-portable-iii)). A disk image on the GoTEK is the practical way to run it, which is one reason the GoTEK comes first; whether SETUP runs from a 1.2 MB image is untested. With a disk in place the 286 can run Doom8088, which has a 640x200 two-colour build for the 286 named `D286CBW.EXE` ([Doom8088 release notes](https://github.com/FrenkelS/Doom8088/releases/tag/v20250108)), and Wolfenstein 3D for CGA ([WolfensteinCGA](https://github.com/jhhoward/WolfensteinCGA)). No frame rate on a 12 MHz 286 was found for either.

### The printer port carries Ethernet at an estimated 60-90 KB/s

The 25-pin printer port is the cheapest route to a real jump in live speed. A printer port of this period sends eight bits at a time but reads only five, so incoming data travels four bits (a nibble) at a time and receiving runs at about half the sending speed ([Brutman, Xircom notes](https://www.brutman.com/Dos_Networking/xircom_pe3.html)). Compaq's guide describes it only as a standard 25-pin interface for a printer and says nothing about reading data back ([Operations Guide](https://archive.org/details/compaq-portable-3-operations-user-manual)), and the community schematic shows the output chip wired permanently as output-only ([parallel port schematic](https://github.com/raymondh2/CompaqPortableIII/blob/main/compaqportableIII_mainboard/Parallel%20Port.kicad_sch), unverified). Everything recommended here works on such a port.

The proven product is the Xircom PE3-10BT, a 1990s "pocket" Ethernet adapter that plugs onto the printer port and connects by network cable to the Pi's Ethernet socket. Its DOS packet driver (the small program that lets network software talk to a network adapter) has a `NON` switch for output-only ports and has been run on DOS 2.1 and later and on an IBM AT. With mTCP it measured **22.8 KB/s receiving on a 4.77 MHz 8088, 53.1 KB/s on an 8 MHz 8086 and 104 KB/s on a 33 MHz 386**, all on standard ports ([Brutman, Xircom notes](https://www.brutman.com/Dos_Networking/xircom_pe3.html); [mTCP performance notes](http://brutmanlabs.org/mTCP/mTCP_Performance.html)). Interpolating, the notes estimate **60-90 KB/s into the 286**, five to eight times the serial ceiling. Both ends then speak ordinary networking, the Pi needs no special hardware or drivers, and the serial port stays free for Kermit. The adapter needs its own 12-volt power supply, because the alternative power cable taps a PS/2 keyboard socket and this Compaq's keyboard uses a coiled cord instead. It has no pass-through for a printer, and it must not be plugged in with the computer on ([Brutman, Xircom notes](https://www.brutman.com/Dos_Networking/xircom_pe3.html)). Asking prices seen in 2026 run from $95 to $382, with a Xircom power adapter listed separately at $39; sold prices could not be retrieved ([eBay listing](https://www.ebay.com/itm/232482077642); [eBay product page](https://www.ebay.com/p/1004526180); [eBay power adapter](https://www.ebay.com/itm/384998749879)).

The budget experiment is a board that gives the Pi its own printer port. Two exist: the open-hardware pi-parport, which must be built by hand, and the Elesar S&P HAT, sold assembled for GBP 38.40 with five in stock on 13 October 2026 ([pi-parport](https://github.com/worlickwerx/pi-parport); [Elesar](https://shop.elesar.co.uk/index.php?route=product/product&product_id=76)). Joined to the Compaq with a LapLink cable (the printer-port equivalent of a null modem), a purpose-written pair of programs should move **40-100 KB/s** (estimate). Nobody has published a working link over either board, and the Raspberry Pi's standard system does not include printer-port drivers, so they must be added ([Raspberry Pi kernel configuration](https://github.com/raspberrypi/linux/blob/rpi-6.12.y/arch/arm/configs/bcm2709_defconfig)). The Elesar board also carries a true RS-232 serial port; whether that avoids the USB adapter's overshoot was not examined. One safety rule is absolute: **never wire the Compaq's 25-pin port straight to the Pi's pin header**. The Compaq signals at 5 volts and the Pi's pins tolerate only 3.3, so a protective buffer chip must sit between them, as it does on both boards ([parallel link notes](../research_notes/Compaq%20Portable%20III%20faster%20links/parallel_link.md)). Ordinary USB-to-printer cables cannot do this job at all, because the Pi sees them only as printers ([pi-parport](https://github.com/worlickwerx/pi-parport)).

### The rear connector is a full expansion bus behind a rare box

The 96-pin connector on the back is the fastest door and the hardest to open. A community schematic traces almost the whole ISA bus (the standard expansion-card slot of the period) onto it, and shows Compaq's expansion unit adding little more than a voltage regulator, an oscillator and one buffer chip ([ISA chassis schematic](https://github.com/raymondh2/CompaqPortableIII/blob/main/CompaqPortableISACHASSIS/CompaqPortableISACHASSIS.kicad_sch), a reading of an unverified design). Compaq's unit provides two slots for 8-bit or 16-bit cards, always run at 8 MHz, and does not accept "skirted" 8-bit boards, whose lower edge hangs down past the short connector ([Product Bulletin](https://minuszerodegrees.net/manuals/Compaq/Compaq%20Portable%20III%20-%20Product%20Bulletin%20-%20First%20Edition%20%28FEB87%29.pdf)); its label reads 2662A and it listed at $199 when new ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compaq_Portable_III)). Nobody sells a modern replica, and the one open design is a schematic with no finished circuit board ([repository](https://github.com/raymondh2/CompaqPortableIII)). Three listings were found: a bare unit at $375 asking, a Portable 386 bundled with the unit sold for $400 (year unclear), and a whole untested Portable III with the unit at $80 ([eBay](https://www.ebay.com/itm/315972513715); [eBay](https://www.ebay.com/itm/127550180353); [PicClick](https://picclick.com/Compaq-Portable-III-2660-w-2662A-Powers-112611112972.html)). The notes expect $300-450 for a bare unit (estimate from those three data points), and a whole machine with the box attached can cost less than the box alone.

Two cards justify the hunt. A PicoMEM is a modern card built around a Raspberry Pi Pico that provides 4 MB of EMS memory, hard-disk and floppy images from a microSD card, and an emulated NE2000 network card over Wi-Fi. Its author lists the Compaq Portable II, a 286, as working, and it costs $59.99 (version 1.1) or $89.99 (PicoMEM 2), both in stock on 13 October 2026 ([PicoMEM](https://github.com/FreddyVRetro/ISA-PicoMEM); [TexElec](https://texelec.com/product/picomem/); [TexElec](https://texelec.com/product/picomem-2/)). No Wi-Fi speed is published for it. A plain wired NE2000-type card is the speed option: mTCP measured 188-191 KB/s on an 8 MHz 8086 and 700-1,005 KB/s on a 40 MHz 386 ([mTCP performance notes](http://brutmanlabs.org/mTCP/mTCP_Performance.html)), from which the notes estimate **200-500 KB/s** through this machine's 8 MHz slots.

The Xircom is the recommended third purchase because it is the only faster live link with measured results on comparable machines and finished software on both ends; the Elesar board is the choice only if writing and debugging new link software is part of the fun. Run the free printer-port test described in the notes before buying either. Some purchases should wait or be skipped. A 16550A buffered serial chip is a drop-in only if the existing chip sits in a 40-pin socket, and nobody has confirmed that; the one published board inventory lists no socketed serial chip ([sharebrained.com](https://sharebrained.com/vintage/computer/compaq/portable-iii/)). The optional internal modem, if one is fitted, is a 300/1200-baud unit, slower than today's cable ([Product Bulletin](https://minuszerodegrees.net/manuals/Compaq/Compaq%20Portable%20III%20-%20Product%20Bulletin%20-%20First%20Edition%20%28FEB87%29.pdf)). A parallel-port Zip drive read at about 6 KB/s on an 8088 in nibble mode ([ToughDev](https://www.toughdev.com/content/2026/03/pic32mz-iomega-zip100-parallel-port-emulator-part-2-hardware-design/)), and the notes estimate 10-25 KB/s on the 286, slower than the GoTEK. Parallel-port sound devices are driven by the 286 itself, so the Pi cannot feed them; sound stays on the Pi's own speaker. Driving the plasma panel directly from the Pi stays off the table: the panel's model and interface are undocumented, and it runs from 200-volt supplies ([Ret.rocks, search excerpt](https://ret.rocks/index.php/one-off-works/compaq-portable-iii)).

Several earlier claims were confirmed with small amendments. NetDrive works as described, with the 32 MB limit and the EtherSLIP 11.8 requirement added. The PicoMEM price range and the expansion unit's two 8 MHz slots were confirmed against today's listings and Compaq's bulletin. The Doom8088 figure of 3-5 fps on this machine remains an estimate: the only report found is 15 fps on a 25 MHz 286 in an unstated mode ([Vogons, search excerpt](https://www.vogons.org/viewtopic.php?p=1317557)).

## What nobody knows until the real Compaq is tested

Every speed in this report is either measured in an emulator or estimated. The list below is what the real machine has to answer, roughly in the order it matters.

| Unknown | Why it matters | How to settle it |
|---|---|---|
| The top serial speed the real 286 holds without losing bytes | Decides whether pictures arrive at 11.5, 5.8 or 3.8 KB/s | Run PILINK; it picks the fastest speed that passes its own test |
| Which serial chip is fitted, and whether it sits in a socket | Decides whether a buffered 16550A can ever be fitted | Kermit's `SHOW COMMUNICATIONS` reports a working 16550A buffer if one is present ([Kermit 3.14 notes](https://www.kermitproject.org/msdoskermit/msk314.zip)); the socket needs eyes on the board |
| How many bytes the owner's FTDI adapter sends after "wait", and whether it holds a genuine FTDI chip | Sets the safe block size for PILINK | Send a counted stream in a loopback test and count the overshoot |
| Whether BIOS mode 40h behaves on the real ROM as the emulators and MicroWeb assume | DOSBox has no such mode, so PILINK's 640x400 path has never run anywhere; no source shows the ROM's own code | Run it on the Compaq. The 86Box emulator models this machine's plasma and could be a first check ([86Box](https://github.com/86Box/86Box/blob/master/src/video/vid_cga_compaq_plasma.c)) |
| The printer port: output-only or not, its address, and whether its interrupt works | Sets the Xircom's switches and rules 8-bit tricks in or out | A two-minute DEBUG test with a 1 kOhm resistor, written out in the [parallel link notes](../research_notes/Compaq%20Portable%20III%20faster%20links/parallel_link.md) |
| Which COM port and interrupt the 9-pin port uses | Both are set by jumpers, so software must not assume COM1 | In DEBUG, `D 40:0 L8` shows the port addresses the BIOS found |
| The system ROM revision | Revision K (29 January 1987) rules out 1.44 MB | Read the BIOS date or the labels on the two ROM chips |
| GoTEK details: the chip inside, whether the Compaq's floppy cable has a twist, the power plug, the bay fit, and whether the machine will start from it | Owner reports range from unpredictable results to a start-up error 162 | Open the GoTEK; try one 1.2 MB image |
| Whether the original 20 MB drive still works, and which CF card and drive type do | Type 42 worked for two owners and only Type 4 for a third | Try the original, then a CF card as Type 2, then 42, then 4 |
| Whether the screen-save timer blanks the plasma mid-video | The default is 15 minutes without keyboard or other activity; whether serial traffic counts is undocumented | Play a 20-minute clip; raise the timer first if it blanks |
| How the plasma looks in motion, and how it shows four-colour CGA | Nothing was found on refresh or smear; the four-level dot pattern is an emulator's model | Look at it |
| Real frame rates: streamed video, Doom8088, Wolfenstein | Every frame rate here is an estimate | The rig's video test, then `-timedemo demo3` and `WOLF3DC.EXE timedemo` on the Compaq |
| SLIP and NetDrive speed on this cable, and free memory with Kermit still loaded (estimated 250-300 KB) | No 286 measurements exist | mTCP's SPDTEST at each speed step; a small test program for memory |
| Whether a PicoMEM works in a Portable III expansion unit | Only the Portable II is reported working | Cannot be tested without the unit |

## Conclusion

The research turns the question from "is the cable the limit?" into "which limit comes next?". On paper the ladder runs about 1 KB/s today, 11.5 over the same cable, 60-90 through the printer port and 200-500 through an expansion slot. Only that last rung comes within sight of what the 286 can paint, so the owner's diagnosis holds at every step below it. A picture format made of plain bytes is independent of the link that carries it, so software written for the free serial step is not thrown away when faster hardware arrives (inference). The one limit no link removes is the 640 KB of memory, and the only purchase that touches it is a PicoMEM behind the rare expansion unit.

The practical consequence is that an afternoon of free tests on the real Compaq is worth more than any purchase made before it. PILINK's own speed test, Kermit's chip report, a resistor on the printer port and the BIOS date together decide whether the serial link runs at 38,400 or 115,200, whether a chip swap is possible, how to set up a Xircom, and which floppy formats work. One unexplored corner deserves a note. The internal modem slot is wired for exactly one serial chip, and Compaq once sold a second-serial-port board for it ([Operations Guide](https://archive.org/details/compaq-portable-3-operations-user-manual)). A small board there carrying a buffered 16550A would give this machine a second serial port with a buffer and no expansion unit. Nobody has published one.
